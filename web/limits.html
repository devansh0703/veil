What Veil hides, and what it does not

Privacy products earn trust in the exact place they are tempted to be vague. So this page is not a footnote. It is a surface, linked from the top of every other one, and it makes three claims without hedging: what is hidden, what stays public, and what a determined observer can still infer.

Provenance of this build: one run on 02 Oct · 14:21 UTC, settlement mode local-ledger-only, chain not-executed. The confidential preconditions were declared, not chain-read and published as privacySource: pool-ledger. This run broadcast nothing — but broadcasting is not hypothetical: the same transfers have been confirmed on Solana testnet and devnet with these proofs, linked from Live on Solana. The limits below describe what the code does and where it has been demonstrated.

The three claims

Amounts and balances
hidden
Every transfer amount is stored as ciphertext, and so is the balance of every confidential account. A validator confirms the transfer is well-formed and non-inflationary without learning the figure. This is Token-2022's Confidential Balances extension, verified on chain — not a mixer, and not a custodian holding your money. Veil's behaviour is to refuse, not to assume: before quoting a price the server checks that the mint carries the extension and that the destination accepts confidential credits. If it cannot check, it answers VEIL-CONF-003 and offers no price at all.
Token account addresses
stay public
Confidential Balances encrypts the fields, not the existence of an account, and not its address. Anyone can see that a given one-time account received a transfer. What they cannot see is how much, or where that account's owner is elsewhere on the graph. This is the claim most privacy pitches get wrong, so Veil states it first — and the merchant ledger prints the destination address of every row rather than hiding it, because hiding it would misrepresent the boundary.
Timing correlation
still possible at low volume
One-time addresses break the direct link between an agent and a merchant, but not every link. If exactly one confidential transfer arrives at a merchant within the minute a specific agent went quiet, an observer who is watching both ends can guess. At high volume that guess drowns; at low volume, as in a demo, it does not. Veil does not claim to hide the occasion of payment, only its amount and its recurrence. No defence is offloaded to "heuristics". This is a real residual, and it is named.

Who can still learn what

The useful question is not "is it private" but "private from whom, and about what". Read the columns, not the adjectives.

An honest boundary, one observer per row. "Knows" is what that party can establish, not what it suspects.
Observer Learns Cannot learn
Anyone watching the account every settled amount The amount. The running balance. Which buyer this was.
The merchant's own key holder Everything about its own account — that is the point of reconciliation. Other merchants' amounts. It holds a viewing key for its own ciphertext, not a global one.
Holder of the mint auditor key It can decrypt compliant transfers under the protocol's audit path. It is a single global key per mint, not a per-merchant one — so it is not, and is not offered as, the merchant reconciliation path.
The RPC provider Your IP, your request timing, which accounts you query. The encrypted amounts themselves — it relays ciphertext it cannot open.
A validator That a confidential transfer occurred, is valid, and is not inflationary. The transfer amount or either balance. Proof verification needs no plaintext.

The rest of the list, in plain language

chain execution: demonstrated on both clusters
The figures on these pages come from a local run that reports its own settlement mode as local-ledger-only — but broadcasting is not hypothetical. Confidential transfers have been confirmed on Solana testnet and devnet, both from the operator's end-to-end run and from an independent payer through the x402 facilitator, with the three zero-knowledge proofs generated locally each time. All four transactions are linked from Live on Solana. The checkout is no longer local-only: serve and the facilitator are also deployed at veil-sable-five.vercel.app, and a payment settled through the hosted facilitator on testnet. What hosting does not fix is the rate-limited public RPC — a busy rail still needs a paid endpoint, and the hosted ledger is per-instance.
where the proofs come from
The proofs are generated on the payer's own machine, by WASM shipped inside the @solana/zk-sdk package. There is no prover service, no RPC round trip, and no fee: a transfer's three proofs build in about 50 ms in Node and run identically in a browser, because it is the same WASM. Nothing about the amount leaves the client to make them — the only inputs are the payer's own key material, the amount, and the destination's public key. That is what makes the client side of this real rather than a promise.
not audited
No third party has reviewed this code. Confidential Balances is an audited chain primitive; Veil's use of it is not. Treat the facilitator as a prototype.
address rotation
One-time addresses only unlink if the buyer actually rotates. An agent that reuses one address is exactly as linkable as a non-private one — the privacy is only as good as the integration.
account pool
Configuring a confidential account needs the owner's signature, so Veil pre-creates and pre-configures a pool of payment accounts rather than configuring one per call. When the pool is exhausted, a naive integration would fall back to a public transfer. Veil refuses instead.
no mixing
Veil never pools funds and never mixes them. There is no shared pot and no custodial balance. The chain sees individual confidential transfers between individual accounts, so the trust model is just Solana's.
a deployment can lie about itself
The preconditions are answered by a probe the deployment supplies. A deployment that answers them from its own pool ledger is reporting a real record, but not an independent one — so the server publishes privacySource: pool-ledger and never calls it chain. Trusting that field means trusting the deployment, which is why it is published rather than kept internal.
what it is for
Hiding what an agent pays, from whom it buys, and how often — from a competitor reading the explorer. That is the threat. It is not a defence against a global adversary who can watch every packet, and Veil will not style itself as one.
