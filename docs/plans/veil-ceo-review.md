## 0A. Premise Challenge

**Real problem.** Companies running AI agents publish their vendor list, spend curve, and
per-vendor price on a public ledger, because x402 settles in the clear. The buyer's
procurement strategy and the seller's book of business are both public.

**Target outcome.** An agent pays per request, the merchant reconciles to the cent, and
neither side's counterparty graph is readable by a third party.

**Do-nothing cost.** The market splits permanently: crypto-native buyers accept the leak,
everyone else stays on monthly invoices and never adopts per-call payments. The protocol
loses exactly the segment that would pay for privacy.

**Does the plan solve the pain, or a proxy?** It solves the *amount* half with a shipped
primitive. It solves the *graph* half with a derivation scheme we invent. The graph half is
the more valuable half and the less proven half. **The plan's own premise 2 says this
correctly, and the review found three places where the implementation contradicts it** —
see Section 1 findings A1, A3 and Section 3 S1.

**CRITICAL GAP A1 — the facilitator currently holds the whole graph.** The design says the
facilitator "verifies settlement ... and records the payment against the merchant's private
ledger," and that the merchant keeps a "derivation index." If the facilitator can resolve an
alias to a buyer, the product has moved the leak from the public chain into one central
database. A judge will ask this in the first minute, and there is no good answer. Remedy:
the facilitator must see only `(payment_id, alias_hash, amount_valid, slot)` and never the
buyer identity. Alias→buyer resolution must require the merchant's own secret.

**CRITICAL GAP A2 — pending vs available confidential balance.** Per the documented flow, a
confidential transfer arrives in the recipient's **pending** balance and needs
`ApplyPendingBalance` before it is available or withdrawable. The plan's step 4 verifies
"settlement" and serves. If verification waits for *available* balance, it may hang or
falsely fail; if it declares success on *pending*, the merchant cannot yet spend it.
Decide explicitly: verify on **transfer inclusion** (slot + signature), then run a merchant
background job to apply pending balances, and surface a `pending` state in the dashboard.

**CRITICAL GAP A3 — the free-tier host choice is unverified against the workload.** The
stack table says "Cloudflare Workers free or Vercel free." Settlement verification reads
chain state and asserts account extensions. Serverless free tiers carry short CPU/wall
budgets and cold starts. This may be fine (verification can be RPC reads rather than local
proof re-computation) or it may be fatal for a 50-payment demo with pacing. Unverified claim,
cheap probe. Probe before the demo is recorded, not after.

**CRITICAL GAP A4 — no idempotency.** x402 is a retry protocol by nature: the agent pays,
retries the request with proof, and the network can hiccup. Without a payment id /
idempotency key, a retry can double-charge or double-serve. A payments product without
replay protection is not shippable, demo or not.

**CRITICAL GAP A5 — nothing asserts the transfer was actually confidential.** The submission's
central claim is a negative: "the judge cannot read the amount." If any code path falls back
to a normal public transfer, the demo looks identical and the claim is false. The plan has no
assertion and no flag. Remedy: after settlement, read the recipient token account, assert the
confidential extension is present and the amount is not a plaintext value, and refuse to
serve if the assertion fails. Add `VEIL_ENFORCE_CONFIDENTIAL=true` so a degraded path fails
loudly instead of silently.

**A6 — enforce the property natively.** The confidential-extension instruction list already
includes `DisableNonConfidentialCredits` ("blocks regular transfers to make account receive
only confidential transfers"). Using it makes the privacy invariant a token-account property
instead of application discipline. Free, one instruction, closes the A5 class at the source.

**A7 — agent funding is unspecified.** The plan funds fees from the faucet but never says how
the agent obtains test dollars, which requires mint → deposit → apply-pending. Without it the
demo cannot start. Add a `fund-agent` script.

**A8 — per-payment rent on one-time addresses.** Each one-time receiving account needs
rent-exempt SOL and `ConfigureAccount`/`ApproveAccount`. 50 payments means 50 accounts. Use
`EmptyAccount` + close to reclaim, and publish the per-payment cost so the model is honest at
scale.

**Data flow — four paths** (happy / nil / empty / error) for the settlement flow:

```
  HAPPY   agent.402 ─▶ parse price ─▶ derive addr ─▶ conf. transfer ─▶ incl. slot
          ─▶ facilitator.verify(pid) ─▶ assert conf (A5) ─▶ serve ─▶ ledger row
  NIL     402 has no requirements ─▶ parse fails ─▶ agent raises "malformed 402"  [handle]
  EMPTY   price = 0 / "" ─▶ is this free? ─▶ plan has no answer  ← GAP
  ERROR   RPC 429 during verify ─▶ ? ─▶ plan has no retry/backoff  ← GAP
```

**Coupling:** the two SDKs are coupled through the derivation scheme, which is why it needs a
single shared package (Q1). Nothing else is newly coupled to the repo (it is empty).

**Scaling — what breaks first.** At 10x (500 payments): devnet RPC limits and dashboard
in-loop account reads. At 100x: one-time-account creation rate and rent, and the merchant's
pending-balance job. Not a day-10 problem, but the cost model should be stated.

**Single points of failure.** (1) The facilitator: if it is down, every wrapped merchant 402s
and never serves. (2) The merchant's derivation seed: lose it and the merchant cannot
reconcile; leak it and all historical graph privacy is retroactively broken.

**Security architecture — who can call what.** Unspecified in the plan. Required:
`serve-gate` (agent, unauthenticated, by payment id), `ledger-read` (merchant only, scoped),
`ledger-export` (merchant only). Any ledger endpoint keyed by merchant id with no auth is a
direct object reference bug (S4).

**Production failure scenario.** Merchant restarts between payment and serve. The agent paid,
the resource was never delivered, and x402 has no refund primitive. The user sees a failed
call and money gone. Decide the product answer (credit the payment id, serve on retry once
the process is back) and say so.

**Rollback posture.** Redeploy the previous build. Durable state that does *not* roll back:
the mint and its auditor key, and the merchant's derivation seed. Time to roll back the code:
minutes. Time to roll back a leaked derivation seed: never.

**Rules applied.** No catch-alls (`catch (e)`, `except Exception`) — the plan must name
specific classes. Every rescue must retry with backoff, degrade with a visible message, or
re-raise with context; "swallow and continue" is unacceptable, and in a payments path it is
how money disappears. Every `← GAP` row above needs a rescue action and a user-visible message
in the implementation.

**Silent-failure priority.** The two worst rows are `ConfidentialAssertionError` (fails to a
false privacy claim) and `DuplicateSettlementError` (fails to a double charge). Both are
invisible to the user in the plan as written.

## Section 3: Security & Threat Model

**S1 — CRITICAL: the auditor key is global per mint, so it cannot be the per-merchant
reconciliation path.** The design routes merchant reconciliation through "the auditor key on
the mint." Solana documents this key as a single optional **global** ElGamal public key per
mint, and every confidential transfer encrypts the amount for sender, recipient, and auditor.
Consequences if we keep the plan as written: (a) if merchants share one test-dollar mint, then
whoever holds the auditor secret can decrypt *every merchant's* transfers, so the
"per-merchant dashboard" is a privacy hole and a false claim; (b) it is unnecessary, because
the transfer already encrypts the amount **for the recipient**, so a merchant can decrypt its
own received amounts with its own key. **Remedy:** merchant reconciliation decrypts the
recipient ciphertext; the auditor key becomes the optional third-party compliance feature
(accountant, regulator, auditor) which is exactly its documented purpose. This simplifies the
design and strengthens the privacy story.

**S2 — CRITICAL: JS/TS support for confidential transfers is unverified.** The wedge is
"one line in an existing npm merchant handler." If `@solana/spl-token` or the JS ecosystem
does not support configuring confidential accounts and building confidential transfers, the
merchant SDK must proxy to the CLI or a Rust helper, which breaks the one-line promise and
changes the architecture. The plan assumes JS support without evidence. Probe on day 1;
answerable in under an hour.

**S3 — WARNING: auditor key rotation only affects future transfers.** Documented explicitly.
The ledger must record which key version decrypted which payment, and rotated secrets must be
retained, or historical totals become permanently unverifiable.

**S4 — HIGH: direct object reference on the ledger.** The dashboard and CSV export read a
merchant's private ledger. If any endpoint is keyed by merchant id without authorization, one
merchant reads another's reconciliation, which is the exact leak the product exists to stop.

**S5 — HIGH: the derivation seed is the entire graph privacy budget.** A leaked or lost
merchant seed either reveals the full buyer graph retroactively or destroys reconciliation
permanently. It must live only with the merchant, never in the facilitator, never in the
repo, `0600` on disk, and be documented as a one-way door.

**S6 — MEDIUM: one-time addresses do not defeat timing analysis at demo scale.** 50 payments
in a narrow window from one buyer to one merchant can be correlated by amount-unlinkability
alone even with distinct addresses. The design doc already commits to the narrow claim
("not trivially correlatable"). Hold that line in the submission; do not upgrade it to
"anonymous."

**S7 — MEDIUM: dependency risk and supply chain.** New npm packages (`@solana/web3.js`,
`@solana/spl-token`, x402 SDK) plus a hosted facilitator. Pin versions, commit the lockfile,
and do not add a paid service. No secrets in the repo; devnet keys generated per deploy.

**S8 — MEDIUM: no audit trail for sensitive operations.** Settlement decisions, assertion
outcomes, and serve/refuse decisions need an append-only log with the payment id, or a
dispute with a merchant cannot be reconstructed.

**Attack surface expansion.** New: a public settlement-verification endpoint, a merchant
dashboard with private financial data, a CSV export, and merchant signing material. Input
validation required on: payment id (uuid shape, length), price (positive decimal, bounded),
alias (base58 shape, length), and every RPC response before use.

per successful confidential payment, and never without one.*

```
  9 │                  │ mark SERVED (2nd) ────┼─▶ serve() AGAIN  │ ✗ VIOLATION
```

**Order that violates the invariant:** two concurrent verifications of the same payment id
(both awaiting RPC) both succeed and both serve. The plan has no mechanism preventing this.
**Prevention required:** an atomic compare-and-set on the payment row (`VERIFYING → SERVED`
must be a conditional write that exactly one caller can win), not a read-then-write.
**Regression proof:** test that fires two verifications of the same id with a controlled pause
between RPC dispatch and return, and asserts exactly one serve.

**Interaction edge cases.** Double-click / retry (A4) — gap. Navigate away mid-payment —
gap. Zero payments (empty state) — gap. Large ledger — gap. Failed/backlogged pending-balance
job — gap. Stale price between 402 and payment — **gap: the 402 must bind the price** (hash)
or an agent can pay a stale price and be served or rejected arbitrarily.

**Findings — Section 4: 12 edge cases mapped, 8 unhandled.**

## Section 5: Code Quality Review

Repo is empty, so this reviews the plan's structure rather than existing code.

**Q1 — CRITICAL: the derivation scheme will be implemented twice.** The agent derives a
one-time address; the merchant resolves an address back to an alias. Two implementations of
one scheme drift, and drift here means the graph silently leaks or payments silently land
somewhere the merchant cannot attribute. Single shared `@veil/derive` package, consumed by
both SDKs, with a property test asserting agreement over random inputs.

**Q2 — naming.** The plan uses "alias", "one-time deposit address", "derived address",
"payment alias", and "receiving account" for related-but-different things. Fix the vocabulary
in one glossary: *alias* = buyer-scoped public handle; *payment account* = the one-time token
account; *derivation index* = the merchant's resolver.

**Q3 — over-engineering risk.** Two settlement paths (confidential and public fallback) means
every verification function branches on both forever. HOLD SCOPE verdict: keep one enforced
path (A6) and make the fallback a test-only fixture, not a runtime mode.

**Q4 — under-engineering.** No input validation on the 402 body, no bound on price, no
pre-flight rent check, no retry discipline. These are happy-path-only patterns in a payments
path.

**Q5 — cyclomatic complexity.** The proposed `verify()` will branch on: pid validity, RPC
result, tx found, mint matches, extension present, amount plaintext?, amount match, slot
freshness, idempotency state, merchant live. That is 10 branches in one function. Split into
`verifyInclusion`, `assertConfidential`, `assertAmount`, `claimServe` (the atomic CAS).

**Q6 — DRY across SDKs.** The 402-parsing and price-checking logic must be shared, not
duplicated between merchant middleware and agent client.

**Findings — Section 5: 6 issues (1 critical).**

**Test that makes me confident shipping at 2am.** The demo smoke assertion: over 50 real
devnet payments, exactly 50 distinct counterparty addresses appear in the merchant's public
history, and exactly 0 amounts are publicly readable. Both are counted and asserted, so the
build fails if privacy degrades.

**The hostile QA test.** Force the code into the public-transfer path (delete the confidential
extension config, or fund a non-confidential account), then run the demo. A correct product
**refuses to serve** and the assertion counter goes to 1. A buggy product serves happily and
the submission's claim is a lie. This test is the single most valuable one in the plan.

**Chaos test.** Kill the facilitator mid-flow and kill the merchant mid-flow; assert no
double-serve, no silent loss, and a visible "paid, not delivered" state.

**Pyramid.** Many units on derivation/assertion/idempotency, fewer devnet integrations, one
E2E demo smoke. That is the right shape. The plan has none of it.

**Flakiness risk (high).** Every devnet test depends on: faucet availability, RPC rate limits,
blockhash lifetime, and proof generation time. Gate them behind a `devnet` tag, make them
retry-aware, and never make the demo depend on a fresh faucet call mid-run.

**Assertion check.** Requirements CS2/CS3 are exact counts and conditions, not preferences:
"amounts unreadable" and "no repeated counterparty address" must be asserted, not eyeballed.
CS5 ("reconciles to the cent") must compare a computed ledger total against the on-chain
decryption, not against itself.

## Section 7: Performance Review

**P1 — the RPC budget is unplanned and is the most likely cause of a failed recording.**
Devnet limits are ~100 requests/10s per IP and ~40/10s for a single method. A confidential
payment needs multiple round trips (account fetch, blockhash, send, confirm) plus facilitator
verification reads plus dashboard reads. 50 payments at ~6-10 calls each is 300-500 calls in
a short window. Without pacing and a Helius free-tier key, the demo will rate-limit mid-take.
Publish calls-per-payment and pace the harness.

**Proof generation latency is unmeasured.** Client-side confidential-transfer proofs are the
slowest step and set the demo's runtime. Measure on day 1 and design the demo around the real
number; if a proof takes seconds, the "50 payments" demo becomes a minutes-long video.

**N+1 in the dashboard.** Reading each payment's account state in a loop will multiply RPC
calls by the ledger size. Batch reads; cache decrypted totals.

**Memory.** ElGamal decryption of N payments in JS is fine at 50 and not at 100k. State the
bound; paginate.

**Cold starts.** Serverless facilitator cold start sits inside the agent's request timeout.
Budget it. If it exceeds the x402 client timeout, the agent sees a failed payment that
actually succeeded. That is a double-charge vector, not just slow.

**Top 3 slowest new paths (estimated p99):** confidential proof generation (unknown, measure),
facilitator verify round trip (RPC-bound), one-time account creation (2-4 txs per payment).

**Findings — Section 7: 5 issues (1 critical: unplanned RPC budget).**

## Section 8: Observability & Debuggability Review

**Required log line per settlement** (structured, one JSON object):
`payment_id, merchant_id, alias_hash, slot, signature, confidential_asserted (bool),
amount_valid (bool), decision (serve|refuse), reason, duration_ms`.

**Required metrics, day 1:**
- `settlements_total{decision}`
- `confidential_assertion_failures` — **the most important number in the product.** Nonzero
  means the core claim is broken. Alert at >0.
- `settlement_verify_latency_ms`
- `duplicate_settlement_attempts`
- `rpc_rate_limit_errors`
- `pending_balance_backlog`
- `one_time_accounts_open` (rent exposure)

**Tracing.** One correlation id per payment, propagated agent → facilitator → ledger →
dashboard row, so a dispute can be replayed end to end.

**Alerting.** Assertion failure (page), facilitator down (page), RPC 429 rate above threshold
(warn), pending backlog growing (warn).

**Dashboard, day 1.** Settlements by decision, assertion failures (must read 0), verify
latency p50/p95, pending backlog.

**Debuggability.** A bug reported three weeks later must be reconstructable from logs alone:
payment id → signature → slot → assertion result → serve decision, all present.

**Admin tooling.** A `veil replay <payment_id>` command that re-reads chain state and
re-asserts the claim. This doubles as the demo's "verify it yourself" link.

**Runbooks.** (1) "Explorer shows a readable amount" → triage assertion failure, halt serving.
(2) "Merchant reports a missing payment" → replay by pid. (3) "Facilitator 5xx storm" →
degrade to refuse-not-serve (never serve unverified).

## Section 9: Deployment & Rollout Review

No database migrations (devnet, fresh state), so the usual migration-safety checks do not
apply. The deployment risks that do apply:

**D-1 — host choice unverified (same as A3).** Probe before recording the demo.

**D-2 — the mint and its auditor key are durable, non-rollback state.** Redeploying code does
not reset the mint. If the mint is created without the auditor key at init, everything after
is wrong; get it right once. Per S1, decide mint-per-merchant vs shared mint before the first
mint is created, because it is not changeable retroactively in the demo.

**D-3 — feature flag required.** `VEIL_ENFORCE_CONFIDENTIAL` (default true in the demo and in
submission builds). A degraded path must fail loudly.

**D-4 — sequencing.** Create mint(s) → configure accounts → fund agent → deploy facilitator →
deploy dashboard → run smoke → record video → submit. Reverse order fails.

**D-5 — post-deploy verification checklist (first 5 minutes).** One real confidential transfer
end to end; assertion returns confident; explorer shows an unreadable amount; dashboard total
matches; CSV opens. **First hour:** 10 paced payments with no 429s; pending-balance job drains.

**D-6 — rollback.** Redeploy previous commit; the chain state stays. Rollback does not undo a
wrong mint setup or a leaked derivation seed — those are one-way doors (see Section 10).

**D-7 — environment parity.** Devnet only, no staging. Say so in the submission rather than
implying production readiness.

## Section 10: Long-Term Trajectory Review

**Technical debt introduced.** (1) Two settlement paths, if the public fallback stays a
runtime mode. (2) A bespoke derivation scheme, which is our own invention with no external
review. (3) A merchant dashboard doing ElGamal decryption client-side in the browser, which
is convenient now and awkward if the ledger grows. (4) Single-builder knowledge concentration.

**Path dependency.** The plan's privacy property depends on merchants adopting our
derivation scheme *and* not publishing a wallet. That is an application-level discipline
unless enforced on-chain (A6). Enforce it natively or the property decays the moment a
merchant adds a normal receiving account.

**Knowledge concentration.** One builder, ten days, no second pair of eyes. The design doc is
good; the runbooks in Section 8 are what a future collaborator would actually need.

**Reversibility: 3/5.** Code and hosting are easily reversible (5/5). The mint's auditor key
configuration is not (2/5 for that artifact). The merchant derivation seed is a genuine
one-way door: a leak breaks historical graph privacy permanently (1/5).

**Ecosystem fit.** Solana devnet, Token-2022, x402, Anchor/JS conventions. Fits.

**The 1-year question.** Would a new engineer understand this in 12 months? The derivation
scheme and the assertion are the two things that must be documented or the next person
misreads "we hide payments" as "payments are anonymous," which they are not (S6).

**HOLD SCOPE note.** No expansions are proposed. The one strategic observation belongs in the
submission rather than the build: if x402 absorbs confidential settlement into the spec, the
moat is the auditor/compliance workflow and the take-rate position, not the cryptography.
The plan already says this. Good instinct; keep it.

**Findings — Section 10: 4 debt items, reversibility 3/5.**
