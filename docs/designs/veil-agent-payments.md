## Problem Statement

x402 is now the default way AI agents pay for things on the web. It reuses HTTP's unused `402 Payment Required` status code so an agent can discover a price, reason about it against a budget, and pay per request in stablecoin — no humans, no API keys, no accounts. The numbers as of late 2026: roughly **200 million transactions**, **$50 billion in volume**, about **150,000 merchant endpoints**, most transactions **under 50 cents**. The standard is hosted by the Linux Foundation and referenced in the docs and workflows of Cloudflare, Stripe, and AWS (Agent Core Payments). **Solana is the leading chain for it** — 38-49% of agent-to-agent payment share, on top of >$15B circulating stablecoins, 400ms blocks, and fees of about a thousandth of a cent.

Every one of those payments is a permanent public record. Who paid whom. How much. How often. At what price per call.

That record is the buyer's strategy. A trading desk whose agent buys 12 data feeds has published its entire research stack to anyone reading an explorer. It is also the merchant's book of business: with public per-client revenue, every enterprise renewal starts with the buyer already knowing your pricing power and your dependency on them.

The fix is half-built, and the gap is documented by Solana itself. Token-2022's **Confidential Balances** hides transfer amounts and balances, and carries an optional **auditor key** on the mint — the authority sets an ElGamal public key, and every confidential transfer encrypts the amount for sender, recipient, *and* auditor, so a designated party can decrypt totals without holding transfer authority. It was **re-enabled on Solana mainnet in June 2026** after a year-long security pause and a Code4rena audit. And the docs say exactly what it does not do:

> "Only transfer amounts and token balances are private. **Token account addresses remain public.**"
> — solana.com/docs/tokens/extensions/confidential-transfer

Two leaks. One primitive that closes the first. Nobody is closing either for agent payments.

**Veil closes both, and gives the merchant a receipt that reconciles to the cent.**

## Status Quo

An operator whose agents buy data today has exactly two options.

**Option one: publish everything.** Run x402 as designed. Accept that your vendor list, your spend curve, and your per-vendor price are on a public explorer. Crypto-native desks accept this because they already live in public. Everyone else does not.

**Option two: leave the protocol.** Prepay vendors by invoice or wire, run a credit balance, meter usage internally, reconcile monthly. This is what most serious buyers do today, and it throws away all three things x402 exists to provide: no API keys, per-request pricing, instant settlement. It also rebuilds the exact friction x402 removed — the Solana Foundation's own webinar notes that manually provisioning keys "can take 30 minutes to an hour" versus a single session with x402.

So the status quo is not "nothing." It is **a monthly-invoice workflow bolted onto a pay-per-request protocol**, which is why the market splits into crypto-native (accepts the leak) and non-crypto (never adopts). The middle — companies that want per-call machine payments *and* a private ledger — has no option. That middle is the product.

## Target User & Narrowest Wedge

**Primary human, role named:** the person who owns the agent budget — Head of Engineering or AI platform lead at a fund, a data-heavy company, or an agent platform, whose agents make hundreds of paid calls a day. Their stake is a board question: *what is our edge?* A public payment graph answers that question for the competition. Their day is spent on infrastructure; their career is spent on whether the desk's research is differentiated.

**Second human:** the x402 merchant's founder. Their pain is the mirror image — per-client revenue is public, so pricing power leaks at every renewal.

**Narrowest wedge:** a drop-in x402 facilitator plus a two-line SDK. A merchant adds one line to their existing 402 handler; an agent pays with no keys. The payment settles on Solana with the amount encrypted and the payer↔payee link broken; the merchant's dashboard still reconciles to the cent by decrypting through the mint's auditor key. That is the entire v1.

**The wedge is the integration, not the cryptography.** Privacy is only interesting here if adopting it costs a merchant one line. Any design that requires a merchant to rewrite payout logic does not get measured traction by Oct 12, and traction is a scored criterion.

## Approaches Considered

### Approach A: Facilitator with two privacy layers (recommended)

Drop-in x402 facilitator + client SDK. Two independent privacy layers, usable separately so a partial build still ships value:

- **Amount privacy:** settlement in a Token-2022 mint with Confidential Balances enabled. Amounts and balances encrypted; the mint carries an auditor key so the merchant's own reconciliation view decrypts to the cent.
- **Graph privacy:** each payment is directed to a one-time deposit address derived from a payment alias (buyer-derived, deterministic, server-verifiable) rather than the merchant's published wallet. On-chain, a merchant's incoming transfers are not linkable to a known counterparty, and a buyer's spend history is not a readable vendor list.

- Effort: L · Risk: Med-High (one primitive is bleeding edge)
- Pros: hits the Insight criterion with a primitive that was actually re-enabled four months ago; both layers are demoable with real devnet transactions; the reviewer-facing "toggle auditor key and the total appears" moment is real, not staged; the integration is one line, which is what makes traction measurable in the window.
- Cons: the confidential-transfer client path (client-side ZK proof generation) is the single highest-risk item and can eat days; the graph layer is genuinely new cryptography-adjacent engineering we have to design ourselves; two layers doubles the surface for a solo builder in ten days.
- Reuses: Token-2022 Confidential Balances + auditor keys (shipped, documented, with a Cookbook), the official x402 SDK and spec, Solana Foundation's Confidential-Balances-Sample as a starting reference, devnet faucet.

### Approach B: Graph privacy only (minimal viable)

Ship only the one-time-alias layer: merchant publishes no wallet, every payment lands at a fresh derived address, buyer's spend history is unlinkable, merchant reconciles via a private derivation index. No Confidential Balances, no ZK proofs.

- Effort: S · Risk: Low
- Pros: three days instead of ten; near-zero technical risk; still closes the *more valuable* half of the leak (the vendor graph) with plain Solana key derivation; leaves four days for the demo, video, and merchant outreach that actually score traction.
- Cons: does not use the fresh primitive, so it scores materially lower on Insight — which is the criterion that separates a winning submission from a well-built also-ran; amounts remain public, so it does not serve the merchant's own pricing-power pain; a judge who knows Confidential Balances exists will ask why it isn't used, and "it was hard" is a weak answer.
- Reuses: everything from A, minus the ZK path. This is Approach A's fallback, and the honest plan is to build A with B as a guaranteed floor.

### Approach C: Veil as merchant of record (creative / long-term)

Buyers pay Veil, not merchants: one confidential transfer to a single Veil address, and Veil settles out to merchants from a pooled account. The merchant sees only "Veil" as the counterparty, so the graph is hidden completely rather than statistically. Veil takes the take rate and becomes the Stripe of agent payments.

- Effort: XL · Risk: High
- Pros: the strongest privacy guarantee of the three — the merchant cannot correlate anything, because it never receives a payment from the buyer; the strongest business, because pooled settlement is a real take-rate position and the buyer relationship belongs to Veil.
- Cons: fully custodial, which is a legal and trust problem, not an engineering one, and a judge will raise it in the first 60 seconds; requires money-transmitter-shaped thinking and a compliance story we cannot build in ten days; pulling it off in the window would sink the demo.
- Verdict: this is the **12-month destination, not the 10-day build.** Include it in the submission's roadmap slide as evidence of direction and market size; do not build it.

**RECOMMENDATION: Approach A**, because Insight is the criterion that distinguishes a track winner from the field, and A is the only approach whose core primitive (Confidential Balances, re-enabled June 2026) is new enough to demonstrate that the founder knows something the judges' other submissions do not. B is the guaranteed floor if the ZK spike fails on day 1-2; C is the pitch's third act.

## Recommended Approach

**Veil** — one-line private payment rails for the agent economy, on Solana devnet.

Working name `Veil`; flagged for a trademark and domain check before anything public ships. Alternates if it collides: `Tacet`, `Redact`, `Quiet`. A privacy product's name should survive being said out loud on a demo video, and "Veil" does.

### The two leaks, and what actually closes each

| Leak | Who exploits it | Closed by | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amount paid per call | Competitors reading your spend; a merchant's buyer-side negotiating power | Token-2022 Confidential Balances (amount + balance encrypted) | Shipped primitive, re-enabled June 2026, needs mint ownership + auditor key |
| Who paid whom, how often (the graph) | Competitors reading your vendor list; a merchant's other clients reading your contract | One-time derived deposit addresses per (buyer, merchant, session) | Nothing off-the-shelf. This is the part we build. |
| Reconciliation and tax | Nobody — this is the internal need | Auditor key on the mint decrypts totals for the authority | Shipped primitive, needs our reconciliation UI |

The middle row is the startup. The docs told everyone confidentiality was solved; the docs also quietly say addresses stay public, which means the more valuable leak was never addressed for payments specifically.

### How a payment works

1. Agent requests a resource. Merchant's handler, wrapped in `veil(price)`, returns the standard x402 `402` with payment requirements pointing at the **Veil facilitator**.
2. Agent's client SDK, also two lines, receives the 402, checks the price against its budget (x402's economic-reasoning property, preserved), and signs a confidential transfer.
3. Funds move from the agent's confidential balance to a **one-time derived deposit address** owned by the merchant. On-chain a viewer sees: a confidential transfer of an encrypted amount, to an address never seen before, from an address not tied to the buyer's published identity.
4. The facilitator verifies settlement, tells the merchant's handler to serve the resource, and records the payment against the merchant's private ledger.
5. Merchant's dashboard decrypts **its own recipient ciphertext** and shows the true amount, the payer's alias, and the running total. Correction (from /plan-eng-review and the design review): each confidential transfer already encrypts the amount for sender, recipient, *and* auditor, so the merchant decrypts its received amounts with its own key. The auditor key is a single global ElGamal key per mint and is *not* on the merchant's reconciliation path; it is the optional third-party compliance feature (accountant, tax authority). Treating it as the merchant's read path is the stale claim this doc used to carry. The public explorer shows none of it.

### What a judge sees (this is the whole submission)

Two browser windows side by side. Left: the agent making 50 real paid calls to two merchants, live on devnet. Right: a Solana explorer on the merchant's wallet showing **amounts that cannot be read** and **no repeated counterparty address**. Then one click: the merchant's Veil dashboard, auditor key applied, and every payment reconciles to the cent with totals, per-vendor breakdown, and a CSV export a bookkeeper would accept.

The demo's job is to make the judge feel the gap between those two windows. That gap is the product.

## Open Questions

1. **Merchant or buyer as the first paying customer?** The design is merchant-first (they have the revenue to protect and a dashboard need), but the buyer has the sharper pain. Resolve with the Assignment below, not with speculation.
2. **Take rate vs SaaS.** 0.5-1% of settled volume, or a flat per-month fee for the dashboard? Volume is the honest model if agent volume keeps climbing; SaaS is the honest model if it's still early. Unresolved because it depends on question 1.
3. **How much graph privacy is achievable without a shielded pool?** One-time addresses raise the cost of correlation but do not eliminate timing analysis at small scale. Do we claim "unlinkable" or the narrower, defensible "not trivially correlatable"? Take the narrow claim.
4. **Do we need Confidential Balances at all in v1 if the merchant's real fear is the buyer's graph?** Day 1's spike answers this better than argument. If merchant pain is graph-dominant, Approach B is not a fallback — it is the right product, and the doc should be revised rather than defended.
5. **Does this product exist in 5 years, or does x402 itself absorb privacy?** If Coinbase and the Linux Foundation add confidential settlement to the spec, our moat is the dashboard, the auditor workflow, and the take-rate position — not the cryptography. Worth stating in the submission; judges reward knowing where your defensibility actually lives.

## Success Criteria

Measurable, and all of it devnet-real:
1. **≥50 real confidential payments** between ≥2 agents and ≥2 merchants, every one with an explorer link. Not simulated, not looped locally.
2. **Amount unreadable publicly, readable by auditor key.** Both demonstrated live; the gap is the pitch.
3. **No repeated counterparty address** across 50 payments on the merchant's public history.
4. **One-line merchant integration**, measured as an actual diff on a stock x402 handler.
5. **Merchant ledger reconciles to the cent** and exports CSV.
6. **≥1 external x402 merchant** who has not met the builder agrees to try the integration. One real yes beats a hundred local calls, and it is the only success criterion that tests the market rather than the code.
7. **Published honest limits**: what is hidden, what is not, and what remains correlatable at low volume.

Hackathon-side goal: place in the Solana track ($100k pool) and/or the general pool ($300k across 20 projects, $30k grand prize). Accelerator acceptance is $250k, and is the actual prize.

### Information architecture

**Dashboard is one screen, three zones top to bottom, plus two persistent controls.** Constraint worship: if a merchant can only see three things, they are the totals, the per-vendor split, and the raw ledger.

1. **Truth strip** (first). Settled-on-chain total in mono at the largest type size on the page; available/spendable balance beside it; the confidentiality badge; and the Public⇄Auditor toggle anchored top-right. Deviations from the obvious: two totals, not one (see states below), because a confidential transfer is not instantly spendable.
2. **Per-vendor breakdown** (second). One row per vendor alias: running total, payment count, last-seen. This is the merchant's business view and it outranks the raw payment list.
3. **Payment ledger** (third). Dense table, newest first: alias · vendor · time · state · amount.

Footer of the dashboard: a reconciled-vs-slot marker and the CSV export (a utility action, deliberately at the bottom so it does not compete with the totals). Payment detail is an **expandable row, not a route** (subtraction).

**Landing is one composition**, not a document: wordmark, one headline, one supporting sentence, one CTA pair, and the two-window demo as the hero asset. No navigation bar beyond an anchor row.

**Two-window demo is a single 16:9 composition**: left = the agent log making real paid calls, right = a Solana explorer on the merchant's wallet, bottom = a live counter strip. The "one click" reveal is a third beat inside the same frame, not a third window.

**Two totals, not one.** A confidential transfer is not instantly spendable. The dashboard shows **Settled (confirmed on-chain)** and **Available (decrypted spendable balance)** side by side, with the in-flight count. **Confidentiality is asserted in the UI, not assumed.** Per prior learning `negative-privacy-claims-need-programmatic-assertion`: the dashboard reads the recipient account, checks the confidential extension is present and non-confidential credits are disabled, and renders an `enforced` badge. If the assertion fails, the dashboard **refuses to display a total** and shows a red refusal state. The negative claim ("the judge cannot read this") is only believable if the product refuses to lie when it is false.

### The redaction metaphor (signature element)

Public view renders every amount as a **redaction bar over the digits**; Auditor view lifts the bars. This maps the product's actual mechanism — encrypted until the holder decrypts — to a visual a judge understands in one glance, and it gives the Public⇄Auditor toggle an emotional payload. One authored motion: the bars lift, staggered, 320ms, `cubic-bezier(.22,1,.36,1)`; instant swap under `prefers-reduced-motion`.

- State is **never colour alone**: every `StatePill` carries a text label and a bordered shape.

### DX decisions (per pass, auto-decided)

1. **Getting started, 3 steps or fewer.** `npx create-veil-merchant my-api` → `veil init --sandbox` → `npm run dev` + a `curl` that shows 402 then 200. Budget: 30s / 30s / 60s, first private payment under 5 minutes. The "one line" claim is restated honestly everywhere as **"a one-line handler diff"**, with setup time stated next to it. (Pass 1)
2. **API surface with defaults.** `veil({ price, currency = 'VeilUSD', alias, facilitator = HOSTED_DEFAULT, onSettle })` returning a standard Express middleware; `veilFetch(url, { budget, maxRetries = 1, onPayment })` returning the resource. Every parameter optional except `price`/`alias`. Escape hatch: any call works against raw HTTP with the documented `402`/`X-PAYMENT` headers for developers who will not take the SDK. (Pass 2)
3. **Error taxonomy with fix + doc link.** Every SDK and facilitator error carries `type`, `code`, `message`, `param` (when applicable), `cause`, `fix`, `doc_url`. Three traced paths: (a) `VEIL-ACC-001` recipient account not confidential-configured → fix points at `veil pool provision` or the sandbox; (b) `VEIL-BUDGET-002` agent budget exceeded → structured body, no silent fallback to public payment; (c) `VEIL-CONF-003` confidentiality assertion failed → **refuse to serve**: the SDK must not fall back to a non-confidential payment when the assertion fails (this is the programmatic form of the honest-privacy rule). (Pass 3)
4. **Documentation set.** Quickstart (copy-paste, real output), Concepts (why addresses stay public), API reference generated from TS types, a Troubleshooting page keyed to the error codes, and a "verify it yourself" tutorial that walks a judge from an explorer link to the CLI check. Docs ship in the repo; the README links each. (Pass 4)
5. **Versioning and key rotation.** Semver from 0.1.0; the mint key version is surfaced in the SDK and the dashboard, and a `key v1→v2` deprecation path is documented (the UI already needs this state). Breaking-change policy for a hackathon build: state it plainly as "0.x, expect churn", with a changelog from day 1. (Pass 5)
6. **Types, CI, and testability.** Bundle TypeScript types; ship a non-interactive `--ci` mode for the CLI (no prompts, exit codes); provide a mock facilitator and a local devnet fixture so tests do not need the live network; document the `@solana/zk-sdk` WASM runtime boundary (Node 20+, browser support unverified). (Pass 6)
7. **License, channel, and pricing honesty.** MIT license on both packages; one public channel (GitHub Discussions) named in the README; pricing stated honestly as "free on devnet; the hosted facilitator is free tier", with the take-rate question deferred to T-1 rather than invented. (Pass 7)
8. **DX measurement.** The sandbox quickstart emits one event on first successful confidential payment (TTHW is measurable end to end); a feedback link on the dashboard; a documented friction audit before the deadline. Any telemetry beyond this is its own decision, not implied here. (Pass 8)
